Directory bias, measured

What the maps miss

The dot maps show who a directory chose to list. Here is exactly how that choice was skewed, measured against the census.

Read this before trusting any dot map on this site. The maps show people a directory chose to list. That choice was not random, and we can now measure exactly how it was skewed.

The measurement

The 1860 census counted every Black Baltimorean by ward. Our 1860 map shows 2,939 of them. Comparing the two distributions gives a representation index: 1.00 means a ward appears on our map at exactly its true weight, 0.50 means it appears at half.

What it says

The map is worst exactly where Black Baltimore was densest. Ward 11 held 9.8 per cent of the city's Black population — more than any other ward — and appears on our map at 0.46× its true weight. Ward 3 held 6.7 per cent and appears at 1.95×. A viewer reading the 1860 map without this table would conclude Ward 3 was a bigger centre of Black life than Ward 11. The census says the opposite.

And the error is not random noise. Sort by Best-anchored % — the share of each ward's residents we could place precisely between two named corners. Ward 3 is 95 per cent well-anchored. Ward 11 is zero. Across all twenty wards the correlation between geocoding quality and over-representation is +0.52. We show most confidently the places we happened to be able to place.

Why

Two causes compound, and both run the same direction.

First, the directories under-recorded the poorest households. Wood's lists 4,251 Black residents against a census count of 27,898. It was a commercial product, canvassed for people worth listing, and it thinned where rents were lowest.

Second, the addresses that survive least well are alley addresses. Our anchor method needs a printed house number on a street we can locate. Alleys often have neither, and alleys are where the densest Black settlement was. So the same neighbourhoods are lost twice: once by the canvasser in 1860, once by the geocoder in 2026.

What this does not mean

It does not mean the maps are wrong about the people they show. Every dot is a real person at a real address, and the anchored ones are placed by the directory's own printed table. It means the maps are incomplete in a patterned way, and the pattern runs against the densest Black neighbourhoods.

The honest use of these maps is as evidence of presence, never of absence. A thin area on a dot map here is not a place where few Black Baltimoreans lived. It may be a place we could not see.

The ward choropleths do not have this problem. They come from the census, which counted everyone, and they are the right layer for any question about how many and where.
