Validation against the 1860 census

Checking the map

We looked people up in the census to see whether we had put them in the right place. Here is what that found, including the bugs.

Nobody had checked whether any of this was right. So we started looking people up in the 1860 federal census, which recorded each person's ward independently of anything we did. If our placements are sound, the wards should agree.

It found real bugs

The first eight lookups matched on only one of three traceable people. That was not noise. It exposed two faults in the highest-confidence placements.

Split streets shared one ladder. North Charles and South Charles both reduce to “Charles”, so one silently overwrote the other and their geometry fused. Thirty-four street names were affected, covering 473 of our best placements. Someone on the north half could be interpolated onto the south half.

Some streets are drawn backwards. On North Caroline the digitised line runs north to south while the house numbers run south to north. The code assumed numbers rise with distance, so it discarded every anchor but the first and pinned people at the wrong end of the street.

Both are fixed. Nothing internal to the data would have revealed either: the ladders were monotone, the anchors were real, and the output looked entirely plausible.

Mostly we are wrong by one ward. Not always.

Nine of eleven mismatches are adjacent wards — boundary distance zero, computed from the ward polygons rather than judged by eye. That is the expected shape of error for a house sitting near a ward line, and it is reassuring.

But two are not. One resident we place in ward 13 the census puts in ward 17, 579 metres away with a ward in between. Another we place in ward 12 sits in ward 10, 371 metres off. Those are not boundary ambiguity. They are wrong.

This correction is worth stating plainly, because we published the stronger claim first. After 32 checks every mismatch was adjacent, and this page said so. Extending to 72 broke the pattern. A clean rule that holds across a small sample is exactly the kind of claim that fails quietly as evidence accumulates, and the honest version is duller: most of our errors are boundary-scale, and a minority are real misplacements of a few hundred metres.

Do the confidence tiers mean anything?

Not demonstrably. Our best tier, anchored between two named corners, matches the census ward 70 per cent of the time. The weakest, street known but position estimated, matches 62 per cent. That gap is well inside the noise at these sample sizes. We cannot presently show that a bracketed placement is more accurate than an estimated one — not the result we expected, and a reason to keep checking rather than to trust the labels.

The real problem is not accuracy

It is that most people cannot be checked at all. Only about a third of those we look for can be positively identified in the census, even with occupation and race filters and generous spelling variants.

That is not evidence of bad placement. It is the same gap the bias page measures from the other direction: the directories and the census are two partial views of the same population, and the people who fall between them are disproportionately the poorest and most mobile. It does mean our confidence rests on a small, self-selected subset — people distinctive enough to trace — and cannot be extended to the majority we cannot verify either way.

How a match is decided

A name alone is not enough. There were two John Ashtons in 1860 Baltimore: a White printer in the ward we predicted, and a Mulatto drayman in a different one. Matching on ward agreement would have produced a confident, wrong link that made our method look better than it is. Matches require occupation or race corroboration, and identification is done before the wards are compared.

Every record consulted is kept — the matches, the rejected candidates, and the search results showing what the alternatives were. A claim about a person in 1860 that cannot be traced back to the page it came from is not worth making.
