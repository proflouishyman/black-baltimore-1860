# Georeferencing the 1860 city directory map

**Map.** *Map of Baltimore, published for the Balto. City Directory.* Surveyed by
William Sides, published by John W. Woods, 1860. Talbot County Free Library via
Digital Maryland, item `tcgc:22`.

- Item page: https://collections.digitalmaryland.org/digital/collection/tcgc/id/22
- Full-resolution download used here: https://collections.digitalmaryland.org/digital/download/collection/tcgc/id/22/size/full
- Accessed 2026-08-08. Local copy: `data/raw/maps/baltimore_1860_directory_map.jpg`

**Is this the full resolution?** Yes. The IIIF service description for the item
(`https://collections.digitalmaryland.org/iiif/2/tcgc:22/info.json`) reports
`width: 5000, height: 4009`, and the largest listed size is the same 5000 x 4009.
The file already on disk is that master. There is no larger scan to fetch.

**Reproduce with:** `./.venv/bin/python scripts/georeference_1860.py`

**Outputs**

| file | what it holds |
| --- | --- |
| `data/work/maps/gcps_1860.csv` | 27 control points, reference coordinates from two independent layers, per-point fit residuals and per-point leave-one-out errors for all three transforms |
| `data/work/maps/baltimore_1860.points` | QGIS Georeferencer control points |
| `data/work/maps/baltimore_1860_coeffs.json` | affine and 2nd-order polynomial coefficients, plus the accuracy summary |
| `data/work/maps/baltimore_1860_georef.tif` | warped GeoTIFF, EPSG:6487, 4.1 MB |

## Headline number

**Leave-one-out cross validated error, affine transform: 26.2 m RMSE, 22.6 m
median, 46.5 m worst point.**

That is the number to quote. It is what you get when the transform has never
seen the point it is being asked to predict, which is the situation every
resident on this map is actually in.

| transform | fit RMSE | LOO RMSE | LOO median | LOO max |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| affine (1st order, 3 terms/axis) | 22.9 m | **26.2 m** | 22.6 m | 46.5 m |
| polynomial (2nd order, 6 terms/axis) | 20.0 m | 25.9 m | 21.6 m | 52.1 m |
| thin plate spline | 0.0 m | 26.1 m | 12.9 m | 78.4 m |

**Read the TPS row carefully.** A thin plate spline interpolates its control
points exactly, so its fit residual is zero by construction and means nothing
at all. Only its leave-one-out column is an accuracy statement.

**The gap between fit RMSE and LOO error.** For the affine it is small, 22.9 m
against 26.2 m, a 14 percent inflation. That is the expected penalty for
spending 3 of 27 points' worth of information per axis on the fit itself. For
the second-order polynomial the gap is larger (20.0 m against 25.9 m, a 30
percent inflation), which is the extra three parameters per axis buying fit
that does not generalise. For the TPS the gap is the whole quantity: 0 m fit,
26.1 m LOO. A reader shown only fit RMSE would conclude the TPS was perfect and
the polynomial was best. Both conclusions would be wrong.

**Which transform ships: the affine.** Its LOO RMSE is statistically
indistinguishable from the other two (26.2, 25.9, 26.1), and it is the only one
of the three that degrades gracefully. The TPS halves the median error inside
the dense middle of the control network, which is genuinely useful, but it
reaches 78 m at Scott & Ramsay, the one point with no close neighbour, and it
has no defined behaviour outside the control hull. The second-order polynomial
does the same thing at the sheet corners. Roughly one resident in ten sits
outside the control hull (see Coverage below), so unbounded extrapolation is
not an abstract worry here. The affine is also very nearly conformal, singular
values 2.1899 and 2.1741 m/px, anisotropy 1.0072, so it is not distorting the
sheet to buy fit.

Incidental result from the fitted affine: the sheet is drawn with its x axis
3.08 degrees counter-clockwise from east, that is, squared up to the Baltimore
Street grid rather than to true north, at about 2.18 m per scan pixel. At the
600 dpi of the scan that is a printed scale near 1:51,500.

**27 points.** Coverage, not count, is what this was designed for.

In scan pixels the points run from x = 1035 (Payson Street, the far west) to
x = 3732 (Luzerne Avenue, the far east), and from y = 671 (Lanvale Street, the
far north) to y = 2400 (Ostend Street, the far south). The map body occupies
roughly x 880 to 4200 and y 490 to 3560, so the control network spans about 81
percent of the sheet's width and 56 percent of its height. The unspanned part
of the height is almost entirely water and Locust Point, which no resident
occupies.

**Is the network collinear the way 1851's was?** No. Baltimore Street is again
the most-used street, carrying 7 of the 27 points, but the comparison stops
there. In 1851 those 7 were 58 percent of the whole network and there was
almost nothing to constrain the north-south axis. Here they are 26 percent, and
the other 20 points are spread over the full height of the built-up area. The
two next most-used streets, Broadway (6 points) and Charles (5 points), both run
north-south and are crossed by nine different east-west streets over about 1,400
pixels of latitude. So the two axes are constrained by different, roughly
perpendicular families of points rather than by one line.

**Coverage against what actually matters.** The convex hull of the 27 control
points covers 14.0 km2, which is 43.7 percent of the 1846-1860 city polygon by
area, but it contains **2,761 of the 3,054 placed 1860 residents, 90.4
percent**. The city polygon includes a lot of empty ground. The control network
covers the inhabited part. The 293 residents outside the hull sit a median 422 m
beyond it and at most 1.3 km, mostly to the southwest, northwest and northeast.
Their positions are extrapolated and the 26 m figure does not cover them.

**Every pixel coordinate was read off the scan.** The method was to crop a
window at 4x to 7x magnification with a labelled pixel grid overlaid, identify
both streets by the name engraved in the street corridor, and take the centre of
the white gap between the facing block edges. Nothing was placed by computing a
transform first. `scripts/georeference_1860.py` carries a per-point note saying
how each one was identified, and the `note` column of `gcps_1860.csv` repeats it.

**What was deliberately excluded.** No point sits on the harbour edge, a wharf,
or the Jones Falls, because the harbour was filled and the falls was culverted
and rerouted. No point sits in the 45-degree grids of Bolton, the Pennsylvania
Avenue corridor or the far southwest, where the engraved streets run diagonally
and the corridor centre cannot be read to better than about 20 px.

**Dropped rather than forced.** Four candidates were abandoned after looking at
them: Wolfe & Pratt (four evenly spaced unlabelled corridors in the crop, no
engraved name near the crossing), Gilmor & Townsend (the two streets do not
cross in either reference layer), Patterson Park Avenue & Baltimore (regular
unlabelled block grid, street not identifiable from the engraving) and Harford
Avenue & Chase (Harford runs diagonally and the cross streets are unlabelled in
that block).

**One point is flagged as lower confidence.** Light & Cross. Light Street itself
is not engraved anywhere near that crossing. It was identified as the second
corridor east of the engraved Patapsco Street. Its LOO error is 18.4 m, near the
middle of the distribution, so it is not distorting anything, but it is the one
point whose identification rests on counting corridors rather than reading a
name.

## Is this circular?

The 1851 script took its control coordinates from the c.1930 HUE file and then
used the fitted result to say how far 1851 sits from HUE. That is circular. A
global least-squares fit absorbs whatever systematic offset the reference layer
carries, so the residuals are structurally blind to exactly the thing being
measured.

This script uses the **modern Baltimore centreline file**
(`data/raw/balt_streets.geojson`) as the reference instead. It is a survey
product with a different provenance from HUE, so the loop is broken in
principle.

In practice it turns out to make almost no numerical difference, and **that is
the single most useful result here**:

> At the 26 control intersections where both layers carry the street, the HUE
> c.1930 position and the modern position agree to a **median of 1.6 m and a
> maximum of 12.0 m**.

Per-point figures are in the `hue_minus_modern_m` column of `gcps_1860.csv`.
(The 27th, Chester & McElderry, has no McElderry Street in HUE.)

Two things follow.

1. Anchoring on the modern file rather than on HUE changes the numbers by
   roughly a metre. So the accuracy figures above are not an artefact of the
   choice, and they are also not circular: they are the disagreement between an
   1860 engraving and an independent modern survey.
2. **HUE is not a systematically displaced layer.** Wherever a street survives,
   it is effectively coincident with modern survey geometry. That has a direct
   bearing on this project's stated worry.

### What this says about the 220-260 m Jones Falls displacement

The project's working assumption has been that the c.1930 HUE base layer is the
largest unquantified error, on the strength of a 220-260 m displacement measured
in the Jones Falls corridor against the 1851 map. This map does not support
that as a general property of HUE.

- HUE agrees with modern survey geometry to a median 1.6 m across 26
  intersections spread over the whole city. A layer displaced by 220 m
  somewhere is not displaced by 1.6 m everywhere else unless the displacement
  is local.
- The 1860 sheet's own worst disagreement with HUE, anywhere in the control
  network, is 37.4 m (`map_vs_hue_m` column, median 22.4 m). Nothing on this
  sheet is 220 m from HUE.

So the 220-260 m figure is either (a) local to geometry that no longer exists,
the culverted falls channel, the filled harbour edge, the streets erased by the
Jones Falls Expressway, in which case it is a real problem but a spatially
confined one, or (b) an artefact of the 1851 fit itself, which had 12 nearly
collinear points, reported training error as accuracy, and drew its control
coordinates from the layer it was being compared with.

**This map cannot settle which.** By design it has no control point in the falls
corridor: the crossings there (Front & Gay, Front & Fayette, the Harrison Street
and Old Town blocks) are drawn on the diagonal Old Town grid and could not be
read to better than about 20 px. Settling it needs a targeted comparison in that
corridor specifically, on a map that renders it legibly. That is the obvious
next piece of work and it is not done here.

## Where the fit is weak, and in which direction

Residual vectors, map position minus reference position, averaged by region:

| region | n | mean east (m) | mean north (m) | magnitude |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| northwest | 2 | -1.7 | -37.9 | 38.0 |
| southwest | 1 | +12.1 | -28.2 | 30.7 |
| southeast (Fells Point) | 2 | +7.5 | -25.6 | 26.6 |
| far east | 2 | -22.1 | -0.9 | 22.1 |
| north | 3 | +4.8 | +19.2 | 19.8 |
| centre-south | 2 | +2.5 | +18.7 | 18.9 |
| south | 3 | -9.3 | +15.7 | 18.3 |
| west | 3 | -7.6 | +16.1 | 17.8 |
| centre | 3 | +6.1 | -13.5 | 14.8 |
| east | 4 | +7.8 | +0.9 | 7.8 |
| northeast | 2 | +1.1 | +1.7 | 2.0 |

This is not noise. It is a coherent north-south bow. The map places the
northwest (Mosher Street between Gilmor and Stricker) about 38 m **south** of
where it belongs and the north-centre (Charles Street at Chase and Madison)
about 19 m **north**, with Fells Point and the southwest also pulled south. The
far east is pulled 22 m **west** rather than north or south. A folded directory
insert printed from an engraved plate is exactly the artefact one would expect
to produce this: paper distortion along the fold lines plus an engraver squaring
the street grid to the plate.

**The three weakest places:**

1. **Northwest, Mosher Street.** Gilmor and Stricker at Mosher both come in at
   38 m residual and 45-47 m LOO, the two worst points on the sheet. They are
   neighbours 53 px apart, so this is a genuine local displacement of the
   engraving, not a reading error at one point. Anything placed in the Mosher /
   Presstman / Townsend blocks of the northwest inherits it. Note the TPS gets
   both to about 6 m, which is what a local warp is for, but only because they
   sit next to each other and vouch for each other.
2. **Southwest, and the Pennsylvania Avenue and Bolton diagonals.** Scott &
   Ramsay is the only control point in the entire southwest quadrant, so it has
   nothing to cross-check it (its TPS LOO error of 78 m is the signature of an
   isolated point). The diagonal grids north and west of it carry no control
   points at all, by choice, because the engraving cannot be read precisely
   there. That whole wedge, Pennsylvania Avenue, Druid Hill Avenue, Bolton, the
   southwest around Ridgely and Stockholm, is extrapolated. **Do not quote 26 m
   for anything placed there.**
3. **Everything outside the control hull.** 293 residents, 9.6 percent, sit a
   median 422 m beyond it. The LOO figure says nothing about them.

Conversely the fit is very good along the Baltimore Street spine and in the
east: Fulton & Baltimore 2.4 m, Broadway & Federal 4.6 m, Calvert & Biddle
6.9 m, Broadway & Lanvale 8.3 m, Caroline & Baltimore 11.6 m.

### Caveat on the far-east points

Chester & McElderry and Luzerne & Monument sit on ground that was platted but
only thinly built in 1860. The engraver may there be reproducing a plat rather
than a survey. They both fit well (11.9 m and 39.4 m LOO), so they are not
distorting the transform, but they are pinning the eastern end of the sheet to
paper streets rather than to anything that had been walked.

## How far this map disagrees with the c.1930 HUE geometry

Per control point, in the `map_vs_hue_m` column of `gcps_1860.csv`: **median
22.4 m, maximum 37.4 m** across 26 points. Because HUE and modern agree to
1.6 m, this is essentially the same quantity as the affine fit residual.

Practically: **re-placing the 1860 residents against this sheet instead of
against HUE would move the typical person about 20 m, and the worst-affected
about 40 m, inside the control hull.** In the northwest around Mosher Street it
would move them about 38 m south. That is under half a Baltimore block. It is
not the 220 m the project has been braced for.

## Honest verdict

**Is this good enough to re-place residents against? Yes, inside the control
hull, and with the error stated.**

- 26 m of cross-validated error is about half a rowhouse block. For a museum
  exhibit that maps people to street segments and blocks, that is fine. For any
  claim about which side of a street someone lived on, or about two addresses
  200 ft apart, it is not.
- The 26 m figure applies to the 90 percent of 1860 residents inside the control
  hull. It does not apply to the Pennsylvania Avenue and Bolton diagonals, the
  southwest, or anything beyond the hull.
- Roughly half of the 26 m is probably the measurement, not the map. At 2.18 m
  per pixel the engraved street corridors are 15 px wide, about 33 m, wider than
  the real streets they stand for, and the corridor centre cannot honestly be
  read to better than about 4 px, which is 9 m. The generalisation in the
  engraving and the reading of it together account for a good part of the
  residual. The map is probably better than 26 m. This measurement of it is not.
- The more important finding is the negative one. The c.1930 HUE base layer is
  **not** displaced from modern survey geometry where its streets survive
  (median 1.6 m over 26 intersections), and the 1860 sheet is nowhere more than
  37 m from it. The premise that the base layer is the single largest error in
  the project is not supported by anything measurable on this map. The Jones
  Falls corridor specifically remains untested here and should be tested
  directly rather than inferred.

**Recommendation.** Ship the affine transform. Attach 26 m as the stated
positional uncertainty for 1860 residents inside the control hull, say plainly
that the diagonal-grid neighbourhoods and everything outside the hull are
unquantified, and treat the swap from HUE to this sheet as a roughly 20 m
adjustment rather than the correction of a gross error. Before doing the swap,
run the same exercise on the falls corridor on a map that renders it legibly,
because that is where the original worry came from and it is the one place this
map cannot speak to.
