Where all of this comes from

Nine primary sources, spanning 1822 to 1868, each linked to the original scan.

Every figure on this site comes from one of the documents below. All are public and linked. Where a page image is shown, it is the actual page the data was read from.

On the language. The section headings quoted here — “Colored Persons,” “Colored Householders” — are the directories' own. They are reproduced because they are what makes this project possible: the publishers segregated these residents into separate lists, and that act of separation is why they can be identified and counted today. The words are theirs, not ours.

The Baltimore Directory for 1822

Richard J. Matchett (Keenan), 1822 · view the original

No separate section. Instead a dagger (†) precedes certain names — 360 of them — which the OCR renders variously as f or t. The meaning is inferred, not stated: this scan's “Directions to the Reader” page, which would carry the legend, is not legible. The reading rests on the flagged entries' occupations (laundress, labourer, drayman, bootblack), their concentration in the alleys, and at least one that is also described outright as coloured. Treat it as a strong inference pending a clean copy of that page. Note also that trailing f. p. and o. t. mark Fell's Point and Old Town, not race. Only 8 of this volume's addresses resolve, so it is parsed but not mapped.

414 residents parsed, 360 flagged

A hand transcription of the “Colored Householders” from Jackson's 1819 directory, giving name, occupation and address. Without it this year would not appear here at all. Addresses are mostly of the form near Bank st, so they place to a corner rather than a block face.

Keenan's Baltimore Directory 1822-23 — AfriGeneas transcription

Transcribed by Louis S. Diggs, Sr., 2000 · view the original

The same volume our own OCR handled badly, read properly by a person. It yields 1,061 entries against our scan's 414, and 230 placed against 8. The transcriber's closing note is worth quoting: “My poor eyes got tired of trying to read those little ‘N side W of whatever street.’”

Wood's Baltimore City Directory — Street Directory

1860, pp. 509–529 · view the original

The key that makes address-level geocoding possible. For every street it prints the house number standing at each cross street, in Left and Right columns, so an 1860 number can be placed between two named corners without ever being compared to a modern one. The flat OCR destroys this table; it is rebuilt from per-word coordinates.

1,521 anchors across 215 streets

Baltimore ward by ward, with white, free colored and slave counts. The only ward-level race breakdown available for 1860: IPUMS does not distribute ward geography in its public complete count. Transcribed from the scan and checked against the printed totals, which reconcile exactly.

Index of Streets and Alleys

Gunby, Baltimore City Archives, 1993 · view the original

A card index of every Baltimore street renaming, and the fix for this project's largest source of silent loss. The streets that failed to geocode were overwhelmingly the alleys the Black population lived on, and most had not been demolished at all — they were renamed. Strawberry became Dallas, Brandy became Perry, Bottle became Dover, Happy became Durham, Honey became Hughes, German became Redwood. 1,460 alias pairs extracted.

1,460 name changes; lifted 1822 from 8 placed to 230

Population of the United States in 1850

Seventh Census, Table II, Maryland report p. 221 · view the original

Baltimore by ward, on the same model as 1860 and on the same ward boundaries, which makes the two directly comparable. Unlike the 1860 volume this PDF carries a text layer. Checked against the printed totals, which reconcile exactly, and that check corrected ward 20's enslaved count.

20 wards; 25,442 free Black, 2,946 enslaved

What we chose, and what it costs

Every source here was picked over an alternative, and each choice buys something and gives something up. This section states the trades plainly, because a reader who knows where the seams are can judge the maps better than one who is told only that they are careful.

The streets are from 1930, not from the period

Residents are placed against the Historical Urban Ecological street file, a digitisation of Baltimore centrelines from about 1930. That is seventy years later than the earliest year mapped here, and it was the single thing about this project we most expected to be wrong.

It is not. Georeferencing five period maps let us finally measure it rather than assume it. At intersections read off the 1822, 1851, 1857 and 1860 sheets, the 1930 street file sits a median of 1.5 to 2.4 metres from a modern survey. Four separate measurements, four maps, two different modern reference layers, all landing within a metre of each other. Where Baltimore's street grid survived, it did not move.

We chose the 1930 file over modern street data for one reason: it still contains the alleys. Strawberry, Lerew's, Happy, Honey. Those alleys are where much of Black Baltimore actually lived, and modern data has lost them. The cost is that it is wrong in the specific places the grid did not survive, and those are known: the Jones Falls corridor, where Front Street no longer exists in any form, and the filled waterfront, where the shoreline itself is not comparable across the period. Those are identifiable rather than spread evenly, which is the useful kind of error.

The 1851 map under the dots is a picture, not the geometry

Switch on The city in 1851 on any map page and Sidney and Neff's plan appears beneath the residents. It is georeferenced from 68 control points read at full resolution, and it is accurate to 12.9 metres tested by cross validation, which the federal standard would report as 22.3 metres at 95 per cent confidence.

Nobody is placed using it. It is there so a viewer can see the city as it actually was, alleys, wharves, ward numbers and all. Placement still runs on the 1930 centrelines, because at 12.9 metres the period map is coarser than the thing it would be correcting. Treat the two layers agreeing as reassurance and treat them disagreeing as a question, not as a correction to the dots.

An earlier version of this georeference reported 56 metres and was wrong in kind, not just in size. Its control points had been read off an image four times too small to show street names, and its error was measured on the same points used to fit it, which is not a measurement at all. Both faults and the numbers they produced are documented in the repository rather than quietly replaced.

Printed census tables over the microdata, where both exist

Two sources give Baltimore's population: the tables printed in the federal census volumes, transcribed here by hand and reconciled against their own printed totals, and IPUMS complete-count microdata built from the manuscript schedules. They disagree slightly. For 1820 the printed volume gives 4,357 enslaved people and the microdata sums to 4,419, a gap of 1.4 per cent.

Where both exist the printed volume wins, because it is what the Census actually published and what contemporaries read. The microdata fills the years the printed ward tables do not cover. Every row on the timeline says which source it came from rather than hiding the seam.

The two agree closely enough elsewhere to be worth stating: on the 1850 and 1860 slave schedules, IPUMS counts 2,949 and 2,221 enslaved people in Baltimore against our hand transcription of 2,946 and 2,218. Three people apart, in both years, from entirely separate documents.

Directories over the census, for the map itself

The census records more people, but it records them by ward, and a ward is a large and awkward shape. The directories give a street and often a house number, which is what makes a dot possible at all. So the maps are built from directories and the census is used to check them.

The cost is severe and worth naming. Directories list householders and proprietors, not everyone in a house. They undercount the poorest and most mobile, who are also disproportionately the people this project exists to show. And when we go looking for directory residents in the census, 63 per cent cannot be found at all. Every accuracy figure quoted here rests on the 37 per cent we could trace. The checking page works through what that does and does not license.

Bulk sources are linked, not mirrored

The full-count census files, the slave schedules and the full-resolution map scans come to about 2.6 gigabytes. None of it is stored in this repository. All of it is public, and data/MANIFEST.md records exactly where each file came from, including the two traps that cost us a day: a download link on the IPUMS site that returns a 404, and an expired login session that serves a login page with a success code instead of an error.

Method and code

Every step is scripted and the repository is public: github.com/proflouishyman/black-baltimore-1860. It includes the parsers, the geocoders, the transcription check on the census table, and a log of every bug found and what caused it.
